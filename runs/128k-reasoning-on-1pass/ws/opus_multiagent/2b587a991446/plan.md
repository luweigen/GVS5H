Model a state as the ordered pair (pos A, pos B); the goal is the shortest path from (S,T) to (T,S) in this configuration space, which is far too large (N²) to search directly, so we must exploit structure. Key derived fact to build on: because σ(a,b)=(b,a) is an isometry of the configuration graph, the answer equals min over "mid‑configurations" {x,y} of D(S,T→x,y)+D(S,T→y,x), and in optimal solutions each half is interference‑free, giving the very clean candidate formula **ans = min over *valid* pairs {x,y} of f(x)+f(y), where f(v)=dist(S,v)+dist(T,v)** (two BFS give f). The whole difficulty is characterizing which pairs are *valid* (i.e. both (x,y) and (y,x) reachable at the naive cost), which corresponds to the known swap mechanisms: rotation around a cycle (cost |C|+2·d(S,C)+2·d(T,C) with distinct attachment points), a single dodge into a free branch at a cut vertex separating S and T (2f(v)+2), and a double dodge into two different branches (f(u₁)+f(u₂), e.g. 2f(v)+4). Impossibility (-1) happens exactly when G is a simple path (no vertex of degree ≥3 and no cycle). Because these mechanisms are error‑prone (e.g. a naive Suurballe/virtual‑node cycle model wrongly gives 6 instead of 10 on the graph triangle{a,b,c}+a–p1–p2 with S=p2,T=p1), every rule must be validated against an exhaustive brute force before we commit to the final linear‑time implementation.
