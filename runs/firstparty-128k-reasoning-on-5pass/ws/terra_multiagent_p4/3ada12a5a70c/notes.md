- **Validation result:** The implementation gives the expected outputs for all three supplied examples: `2`, `1`, and `1`.
- **Brute-force validation:** Exhaustive comparison is appropriate on small squares: enumerate every boundary-point subset, every `k`-combination, compute its direct pairwise Manhattan minimum, and compare the optimum against `maxDistance`. The perimeter reduction and greedy circular checker agree with direct brute force for small-side adversarial configurations, including sparse point sets, all boundary points, corner-heavy sets, and points concentrated on one or two sides.
- **Upper bound:** Since `k >= 4`, the optimum is at most `side`. If four boundary points had all pairwise Manhattan distances greater than `side`, no two could belong to the same half-open side. Thus there would be one point per side. Writing each point's offset from its side's clockwise starting corner gives strict inequalities around the square forming an impossible cycle.
- **Perimeter encoding:** Use clockwise positions in `[0, 4*side)`: bottom `x`; right `side+y`; top `3*side-x`; left `4*side-y`. Testing bottom first, then right, top, left assigns corners consistently. Incident-side formulas give identical perimeter coordinates at every corner.
- **Threshold equivalence:** Only thresholds `d <= side` are searched. For points on the same side, Manhattan distance equals perimeter separation. For adjacent sides, Manhattan distance equals the perimeter arc through their shared corner, which is the shorter circular arc. For opposite sides, both Manhattan distance and both perimeter arcs are at least `side`, so both automatically satisfy every searched threshold. Therefore Manhattan separation at least `d` is equivalent to circular perimeter separation at least `d`.
- **Feasibility:** Duplicate sorted perimeter positions with an added perimeter. A two-pointer pass computes `nxt[i]`, the first later point at distance at least `d`. For each possible first point, repeatedly following `nxt` selects subsequent points greedily. Greedy is sufficient because choosing an earlier valid next point cannot reduce options for future selections. The last selected point must additionally leave wrap-around gap at least `d`.
- **Complexity:** Each feasibility check is `O(n*k)` time and `O(n)` memory. Binary search uses `O(log side)` checks, yielding at most about `15000 * 25 * 30` greedy transitions.
