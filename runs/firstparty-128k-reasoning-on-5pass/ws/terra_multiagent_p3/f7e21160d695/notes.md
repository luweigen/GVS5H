- **MST bottleneck property:** For every two vertices \(x,y\), the minimum possible maximum-edge weight over graph paths equals the maximum edge weight on the unique \(x\)-\(y\) path in any minimum spanning tree. One proof uses Kruskal: immediately before weight \(w\) edges are considered, vertices are connected exactly when their bottleneck distance is below \(w\); the first threshold at which they become connected is their minimax value. Thus it is sufficient to solve the matching problem on a Kruskal MST.
- **Balance representation:** At each vertex, store `count(A occurrences) - count(B occurrences)`. This handles duplicate values correctly. The condition that no vertex belongs to both sequences is not essential to the arithmetic, but guarantees their supports are disjoint. A positive component balance means that many unmatched A occurrences remain; a negative balance means unmatched B occurrences remain.
- **Kruskal DSU algorithm:** Sort graph edges by nondecreasing weight. Use DSU as Kruskal does. Whenever an edge of weight \(w\) successfully joins roots with balances \(x,y\), if their signs differ, exactly `min(abs(x), abs(y))` unmatched A/B pairs can be matched between the two components. Add \(w\) times this number to the answer. Replace the merged component balance by \(x+y\). Ignore cycle edges. Successful unions form an MST, so this simultaneously constructs the needed tree and performs the matching calculation.
- **Why a union charge is necessary:** Before a successful union at weight \(w\), its two DSU components have no tree path between them using only previously selected edges, all of which have weight at most \(w\). Any pair with one endpoint in each component has bottleneck at least \(w\), because they are disconnected at all smaller processed weights. Once joined by this edge, such a pair has a path whose maximum edge is exactly \(w\). Therefore every cross-component opposite surplus pair that is matched now has unavoidable and attainable cost \(w\).
- **Why all cancellable surplus should be matched immediately:** Suppose balances have opposite signs. Keeping an unmatched A in one side and B in the other for a later merge cannot reduce their cost: any later connection has weight at least \(w\). Matching them at the current merge costs \(w\), which is optimal. After these cancellations, only the net balance \(x+y\) matters for all future edges, since all later paths from the merged component to outside have a bottleneck determined by a later edge and do not depend on which internal occurrence supplied the surviving surplus.
- **Inductive correctness invariant:** After any prefix of successful Kruskal unions, every DSU component has internally matched as many opposite occurrences as possible at the smallest available bottleneck levels, its stored balance is precisely its unmatched A-minus-B count, and the accumulated answer is the minimum cost of these forced internal matches. Initially this is immediate for singleton vertices. At a union, previously internal matches remain optimal; the only newly possible pairs are between the two components. The preceding arguments show that all possible such opposite pairs must and can be charged at exactly \(w\), and the resulting surplus is \(x+y\). Hence the invariant is preserved. At completion, total balance is zero because both sequences have length \(K\), so every occurrence is matched and the answer is globally optimal.
- **Equal edge weights:** Processing equal-weight successful unions in arbitrary order is safe. Consider all DSU components that existed before weight \(w\), with balances \(b_1,\ldots,b_t\), that become connected through selected weight-\(w\) edges. Any matching newly enabled among them has cost exactly \(w\). Regardless of the spanning-tree edge order used within this group, the total number cancelled is \((\sum_i |b_i| - |\sum_i b_i|)/2\), because each cancellation reduces the total absolute balance by two and the final balance is the fixed sum. Thus both the charge at this weight and the outgoing balance are order-independent.
- **Complexity:** Sorting costs \(O(M\log M)\). DSU operations cost \(O(M\alpha(N))\). Memory usage is \(O(N+M)\). Python integers safely hold the answer.
