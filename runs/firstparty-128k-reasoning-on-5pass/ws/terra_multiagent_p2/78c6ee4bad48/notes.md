- **Gap transformation:** Let sorted coordinates be \(y_1<\cdots<y_N\), with gaps \(d_j=y_{j+1}-y_j\). For an operation at piece index \(i\), the four involved coordinates have consecutive gaps \(d_i,d_{i+1},d_{i+2}\). After reflecting the two inner points, their new coordinates are \(y_i+d_{i+2}\) and \(y_i+d_{i+1}+d_{i+2}\). Therefore the affected gaps become \((d_{i+2},d_{i+1},d_i)\): the operation swaps gaps \(i\) and \(i+2\), leaving the middle gap unchanged.
- **Reachability:** Every operation swaps two consecutive members of one parity class of gap indices: indices \(i,i+2\) are adjacent among either odd-indexed gaps or even-indexed gaps. Since adjacent swaps generate every permutation, gaps at odd indices can be arbitrarily permuted among odd positions, independently of gaps at even indices. No operation changes a gap's parity class.
- **Objective formula:** Reconstructing coordinates gives \(y_k=y_1+\sum_{j<k}d_j\). Hence \(\sum_{k=1}^N y_k=N y_1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(N-j)d_j\). The first coordinate is invariant because all operations keep the selected outer points fixed and never affect the global leftmost point.
- **Optimization:** Within either parity class, positions are in increasing gap-index order while weights \(N-j\) strictly decrease. By the rearrangement/exchange argument, to minimize the weighted sum, assign smaller gaps to earlier positions with larger weights. Thus sort odd-indexed gaps ascending and sort even-indexed gaps ascending, then place each sorted list back into its corresponding parity positions.
- **Complexity:** Computing gaps and the final weighted sum is \(O(N)\); sorting both parity lists costs \(O(N\log N)\). Memory usage is \(O(N)\). Python integers safely handle all sums.
- **Sample checks:** For sample 1, gaps are \([4,2,3]\); odd gaps sort to \([3,4]\), yielding gaps \([3,2,4]\) and total \(21\). For sample 2, gaps are \([1,5,4,4,2]\); sorted parity placement gives \([1,4,2,5,4]\), whose weighted sum is \(41\).
