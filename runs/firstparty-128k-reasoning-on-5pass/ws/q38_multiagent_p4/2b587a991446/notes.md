
## ideation
The core difficulty is that the natural state space is the ordered pair of vertices occupied by A and B, which is quadratic in N and far too large for N up to 2e5. More importantly, the answer is not determined by the ordinary distance between S and T. On a simple path the swap is impossible even when S and T are adjacent, while on a triangle or a longer cycle the minimum can be the length of a cycle or a cycle-related detour. In trees with branches, the cost depends on whether the useful branch is internal to the S-T path or at an endpoint. Thus the problem is really about finding where the two tokens can “pass” each other: at a vertex with enough incident branches, inside a biconnected cyclic component, or in an off-path cyclic structure that requires a detour.

A correct solution must separate two parts: global travel cost, which can be handled by shortest-path distances from S and T, and local passing cost, which depends on the local graph structure around the place where the tokens avoid collision. The main pitfalls are that local passing cost is not always just “girth” or “degree >= 3”; endpoint cases, off-path blocks, and blocks entered/exited at two different vertices can all change the constant or the required cycle length. Also, if using biconnected components, the local subproblem of finding the shortest cycle through one or two specified vertices is nontrivial and must be done efficiently over all relevant components.
