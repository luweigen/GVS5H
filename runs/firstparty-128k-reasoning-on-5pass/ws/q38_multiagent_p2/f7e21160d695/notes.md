- **Core idea:** f(x, y) is the smallest threshold T such that x and y are connected using only edges of weight at most T. Processing edges in increasing weight therefore builds a laminar family of connected components, exactly like Kruskal.
- **State:** For each current component, keep net = (#A terminals) - (#B terminals), with multiplicities. Non-terminal vertices start with net 0.
- **Greedy step:** When an edge of weight w merges two components with nets n1 and n2, if the signs differ, match min(abs(n1), abs(n2)) opposite terminals at cost w and add that to the answer. The merged component has net n1 + n2. If the signs are the same, no cross match is possible or needed at this weight.
- **Optimality:** For any threshold T, in each component at least |net| terminals must be matched outside that component, so at least (sum |net|) / 2 pairs have cost greater than T. The greedy process leaves exactly that many unmatched terminals after all edges of weight at most T are processed, so it attains the pointwise lower bound on the tail count of costs. Hence the total sum is minimized.
- **Equal weights:** Processing same-weight edges one by one is safe. Within a batch of weight w, the total number of surplus units canceled is min(total positive, total negative), independent of union order, and all such matches have the same cost w.
- **Implementation details:** Sort edges by weight, use a disjoint set union with path halving and union by size. Python integers are sufficient for the answer, which is at most about 2e14. Deleting the parsed input list before the DSU phase reduces memory.
- **Samples:** Sample 1 accumulates 1 + 2 + 5 = 8. Sample 2 accumulates 1 + 2 = 3.
- **Edge cases:** Duplicate A or B vertices are handled by counts. If a vertex appeared in both sequences, net cancellation would correspond to zero-cost matching. The graph is connected, so the final total net is zero.
