- **Core decomposition:** For a fixed middle index k with value m, choose two indices from the left and two from the right. Let a and b be the counts of m on the left and right, and A and B be the counts of non-m values on the left and right. Let x and y be the numbers of selected outer m values from the left and right. If x + y >= 2, then m appears at least three times total and is automatically the unique mode. If x + y == 1, then m appears exactly twice, so the three selected non-m values must be pairwise distinct. If x + y == 0, m appears only once and can never be the unique mode.
- **Automatic valid cases:** Count all choices with at least two outer copies of m by summing C(a, x) * C(A, 2 - x) * C(b, y) * C(B, 2 - y) over x, y in {0, 1, 2} with x + y >= 2. Repeats among non-m values are harmless in this case because m already has count at least 3.
- **Exactly one outer m:** If the single extra m is on the left, choose it in a ways, choose one non-m left index, and choose two non-m right indices whose values are distinct from each other and from the left value. The symmetric case is b times the same count with sides swapped.
- **Cross-side helper:** For frequency dicts X and Y, S(X, Y) counts pairs consisting of one index from X and two indices from Y such that all three values are distinct. If totalY = |Y| and dp = C(totalY, 2) - sum_v C(Y_v, 2), then for a value v on the X side, valid Y-pairs are dp - Y_v * (totalY - Y_v). Therefore S(X, Y) = |X| * dp - sum_v X_v * Y_v * (totalY - Y_v).
- **Complexity:** For each of O(n) middle indices, rebuild left and right frequency dictionaries in O(n) and evaluate two cross-side counts in O(n). Total time is O(n^2), which is easily within n <= 1000. Memory is O(n) per middle index.
- **Edge cases:** Only middle indices with at least two indices on both sides are considered, i.e. 2 <= k <= n - 3. All-equal arrays reduce to sum_k C(k, 2) * C(n - 1 - k, 2) = C(n, 5). All-distinct arrays give 0 because a = b = 0. Length 5 is handled naturally with exactly one left pair and one right pair. Negative and large values are safe as dictionary keys.
- **Verification:** The provided examples match: all six 1s gives 6; [1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4] gives 4, with middle indices 2 and 3 each contributing 2; all distinct length 9 gives 0. Manual brute-force enumeration of all 5-index subsequences on small arrays, including n = 5 and n = 6 random duplicate cases, all-equal, all-distinct, and negative values, matches the formula. The x + y cases are disjoint, and each 5-subsequence has a unique middle index, so there is no double counting.
