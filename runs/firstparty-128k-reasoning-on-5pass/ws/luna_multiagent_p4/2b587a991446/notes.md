- **Characterization:** A legal swap exists exactly when there are two internally vertex-disjoint paths from \(S\) to \(T\). Their union contains a simple cycle through \(S,T\).
- **Optimality:** Any legal sequence induces two walks from \(S\) to \(T\). Removing redundant portions and examining the first point where the token order is reversed yields two internally vertex-disjoint \(S\)-\(T\) paths. Hence the number of operations is at least the sum of their lengths. Conversely, traversing those two paths in opposite directions performs the swap in exactly that sum.
- **Reduction:** The answer is therefore the minimum total length of two internally vertex-disjoint \(S\)-\(T\) paths, equivalently the shortest cycle containing both \(S\) and \(T\).
- **Flow construction:** Split every internal vertex into `vin -> vout` with capacity 1 and cost 0. For each undirected edge \(u-v\), add directed arcs `uout -> vin` and `vout -> uin`, each with capacity 1 and cost 1. Send two units from `Sout` to `Tin`. Omitting split arcs for \(S,T\) gives them capacity 2.
- **Algorithm:** Successive shortest augmenting paths with Dijkstra and potentials, only twice. The residual graph supports rerouting the first path when finding the second.
- **Complexity:** \(O((N+M)\log N)\) time and \(O(N+M)\) memory.
- **Validation:** A path has only one \(S\)-\(T\) path, so the flow is less than 2 and the answer is \(-1\). A triangle with \(S,T\) adjacent gives paths of lengths 1 and 2, answer 3. A square with opposite terminals gives 2 and 2, answer 4. The samples produce 3, \(-1\), and 4 respectively.
