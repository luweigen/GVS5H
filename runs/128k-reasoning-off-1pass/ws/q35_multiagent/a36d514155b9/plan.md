The key insight is to use linearity of expectation (or rather, linearity of summation) by considering the contribution of each pair of cells to the total sum. For any two distinct cells $c_1$ and $c_2$, we calculate how many valid arrangements have pieces at both $c_1$ and $c_2$. If there are $N = m \times n$ total cells, the number of ways to place $k$ pieces such that two specific cells are occupied is $\binom{N-2}{k-2}$. The Manhattan distance between $c_1$ and $c_2$ is $|x_1 - x_2| + |y_1 - y_2|$. We sum this distance over all pairs of cells, then multiply by $\binom{N-2}{k-2}$. To efficiently compute the sum of Manhattan distances over all pairs of cells, we can separate the x and y components. The sum of $|x_i - x_j|$ over all pairs can be computed by sorting the x-coordinates (which are just $0, 0, ..., 1, 1, ..., m-1, m-1$ repeated $n$ times each) and using a prefix sum technique. Similarly for y-coordinates. The final answer is $(\text{sum\_x} + \text{sum\_y}) \times \binom{N-2}{k-2} \pmod{10^9+7}$.
