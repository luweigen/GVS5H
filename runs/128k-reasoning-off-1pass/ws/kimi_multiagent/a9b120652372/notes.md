The core difficulty: pieces move simultaneously toward a chosen square each operation, so all pieces move at most 1 per step, and crucially pieces cannot cross each other (order is preserved). Pieces are identical, so the j-th piece in initial order must end at the j-th target square (in sorted order). After k operations, piece starting at p can be anywhere in [p-k, p+k], but the simultaneous-move constraint is stronger than independent movement: in one operation, all pieces move toward the same point i, meaning pieces left of i move right, right of i move left, and the piece at i stays. However, a known characterization: a configuration is reachable in k operations iff we can assign each target Q_j to P_j with |P_j - Q_j| ≤ k AND the movement is "consistent" — actually the precise condition involves that the sequence of moves can be decomposed. The standard approach: binary search on k, and for each k check feasibility greedily — each piece j must occupy Q_j, reachable iff |P_j - Q_j| ≤ k is necessary but not sufficient; the real check is whether there's a sequence of k "attraction" operations achieving the displacement profile. Each operation changes the position vector by moving all pieces toward one point; the net effect over k operations must match per-piece displacement d_j = Q_j - P_j, where consecutive pieces' displacements must satisfy order preservation (Q_j strictly increasing is given). The check per k likely reduces to verifying that displacements d_j ∈ [-k, k] and that the displacement sequence can be realized — e.g., the number of "sign changes" or the structure of +1/-1/0 moves per step. Pitfall: pieces at the target square i don't move, so per-step each piece moves +1, -1, or 0, but the choice is constrained globally (all pieces move toward the same i). The minimum k answer for samples: 3, -1, 5.

**Derivation of the feasibility condition for fixed k:**

*Setup.* Pieces are identical, order is preserved (a piece at square j moves to j±1 or stays; two pieces can never cross because they move simultaneously toward the same pivot — if piece x is left of piece y, after one step x' ≤ y'). Pieces may stack. So with m pieces (sorted starts P) and t targets (sorted B-1 positions Q), any valid final configuration corresponds to a **monotone surjective assignment**: distinct pieces g₁ < g₂ < … < g_t with piece g_s ending at Q_s, and every remaining piece ending on some target square (it cannot stop on a B-0 square). Necessary: m ≥ t, each matched pair satisfies |P[g_s] − Q_s| ≤ k (a piece moves ≤ 1 per operation), and every piece has some target within distance k.

**(1) Sufficiency of |d_j| ≤ k under monotone assignment.** Claim: given any monotone assignment with per-piece displacements d_j = target(j) − P_j satisfying |d_j| ≤ k, there exists a sequence of k global-pivot operations realizing it. Proof sketch (constructive): process operations one at a time. At each step, let S⁺ = pieces still needing to move right, S⁻ = pieces still needing to move left. Because the assignment is monotone and pieces never cross, at any point the pieces needing right-moves form a prefix and those needing left-moves form a suffix of the current order (a piece only needs to move toward its assigned target; pieces between two settled pieces are themselves settled or moving toward a target between them). Choose pivot i strictly between the last right-needer and the first left-needer (or at an extreme if only one side is nonempty): then every S⁺ piece moves +1, every S⁻ piece moves −1, others stay or move harmlessly toward already-reached targets... The subtle case is a piece that would overshoot; we avoid this by choosing the pivot beyond its target when it's at distance 0. Since |d_j| ≤ k, k steps suffice: each step reduces every remaining |d_j| by exactly 1. Hand-traces (d=[1,−1] via pivot between; d=[2,−1] via pivot at the right target then holding) confirm the construction. The key point: with a monotone assignment the "move right" set is always a prefix and "move left" set a suffix, so a valid pivot always exists. Hence the condition is exactly: **∃ monotone surjective assignment with all |d_j| ≤ k.**

**(2) Greedy matching rule.** Existence of the increasing sequence g₁<…<g_t with |P[g_s] − Q_s| ≤ k is checked by the classic earliest greedy: for each target s in order, take the leftmost unused piece in [Q_s−k, Q_s+k]. Exchange argument: if an optimal matching uses a later piece for target s, swapping in the earlier available piece keeps all constraints (later targets need pieces ≥ current, and the freed later piece is strictly to the right, still usable). Skipping pieces with P_j < Q_s−k is safe because they cannot reach target s nor any later target (targets increase), so they must already have been matched to earlier targets — if they weren't, the separate "absorption" pass catches infeasibility. The absorption pass (every piece within k of *some* target) combined with monotonicity is valid: a piece lying between matched pieces g_s and g_{s+1} can only end at Q_s or Q_{s+1} without crossing, and the contiguous-reach property guarantees that if it's within k of any target, it's within k of one of those two.
