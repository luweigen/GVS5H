The problem asks us to select $K$ disjoint pairs of cakes to maximize the sum of their prices, where the price of a pair is the maximum of the sums of their respective beauty, tastiness, or popularity values. Since the price function is the maximum of three linear functions, we can iterate through each of the three attributes (beauty, tastiness, popularity) as the "dominant" attribute for the pairs. For a fixed dominant attribute, say beauty, we want to pair cakes such that the sum of their beauty values is maximized, while ensuring the constraint that the pair's price is indeed determined by beauty (or at least not less than the other attributes, which is naturally handled if we simply sum the max possible beauty pairs and check if the assumption holds globally). However, a more robust approach for this specific structure is to realize that the optimal solution will be dominated by one of the three attributes. We can solve the problem for each attribute independently: sort the cakes by that attribute in descending order and greedily pair adjacent elements (indices $2i$ and $2i+1$). We then take the maximum result among the three scenarios. This works because if the optimal solution is dominated by attribute $X$, the greedy strategy on $X$ will find a set of pairs with the maximum possible sum of $X$ values, which will be $\ge$ the sum of any other valid pairing's $X$ values, and since the price is $\max(X, Y, Z)$, maximizing the dominant component is sufficient.
