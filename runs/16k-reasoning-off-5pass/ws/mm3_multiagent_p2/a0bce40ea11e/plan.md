As argued, the sum is (-1)^K times the sum of coefficients of monomials where each variable's exponent is a multiple of p-1. This is exactly the evaluation of the polynomial (A+Z)^p at Z = 1? Not exactly.

But note that in the quotient ring R = F_p[Z] / (Z_{i,j}^{p-1} - 1 for all zero positions), the condition that exponents are multiples of p-1 is equivalent to saying that the monomial is the constant term. So we want the constant term of (A+Z)^p in R. But (A+Z)^p is a matrix whose entries are polynomials. We want the constant term (i.e., the coefficient of the monomial with all exponents 0) of each entry. However, in R, Z_{i,j}^{p-1} = 1, so any monomial can be reduced to one with exponents in {0,...,p-2}. The constant term in R is the coefficient of the monomial with all exponents 0. But in the original polynomial, the monomial with all exponents 0 corresponds to setting all Z=0. That gives A^p. But we also want monomials where some variables have exponent p-1. In R, since Z^{p-1}=1, those monomials are also constant. So the constant term in R is the sum of all coefficients of monomials in the original polynomial where each variable's exponent is either 0 or a multiple of p-1. Since the maximum exponent is p, the only multiple is p-1. So the constant term in R is exactly the sum we want. Therefore, S = (-1)^K * (constant term of (A+Z)^p in R).
