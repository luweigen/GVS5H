## worker: Implement the full solution in Python: parse input
- **Optimality**: Cost per product is convex (k²·P_i), so marginal costs (2k−1)·P_i are increasing. The optimal purchase for K total units is the K cheapest marginal units across all products (greedy exchange argument: any optimal purchase missing a cheaper available marginal can swap to reduce cost).
- **Threshold formulation**: For a marginal threshold X, product i contributes k_i = ⌊(⌊X/P_i⌋+1)/2⌋ units (all k with (2k−1)P_i ≤ X). The sum of the first k_i marginals equals k_i²·P_i exactly (sum of odd numbers).
- **Binary search**: Find the maximal integer X with cost(X) ≤ M. hi = 2·isqrt(M·pmin)+10 is guaranteed infeasible since the cheapest product alone would cost ≥ hi²/(4·pmin) > M. lo = 0 is always feasible (cost 0 ≤ M since M ≥ 1). ~47 iterations × O(N) with early break when cost exceeds M (keeps big-int arithmetic small).
- **Tie/remainder handling**: At maximal X, let cnt = Σk_i, cost = Σk_i²P_i, R = M − cost. The next cheapest marginal is v = min_i (2k_i+1)P_i. Each additional unit costs ≥ v, and at least one product offers units at exactly v. If t = #{products with next marginal = v}, buying all t would cost t·v, which would contradict maximality of X (cost at threshold v would still be ≤ M), so R//v < t and exactly R//v extra units can be bought. Answer = cnt + R//v.
- **Verified against Sample 1 by hand**: X=4, cnt=3, cost=8, R=1, v=5 → answer 3. ✓
- Supply of 10^100 per product is effectively infinite (answer ≤ ~2·10^14), so no cap needed.
