
## ideation
Core difficulty: The game ends exactly when the last unvisited index is first chosen. Total moves = N + (extra moves used), where extra moves available = sum(A_i - 1). Players control parity by choosing whether to "waste" moves on already-visited indices or to visit new indices. The player who is forced to make the last first-visit loses control of parity.

Key observations to develop:
- Each index i contributes 1 forced first-visit move plus (A_i - 1) optional "stalling" moves.
- Once an index is visited, its remaining A_i - 1 moves are pure filler (like a Nim heap of "passes" that both players share).
- This resembles a parity game: think of it as — there are N "new index" tokens and M = sum(A_i - 1) "repeat" tokens. Players alternately take tokens; the game ends when the last "new index" token is taken, and that player wins. Players choose which token type to take each turn (repeat tokens are interchangeable, new-index tokens are interchangeable for parity purposes — BUT with a subtlety: you can only take a repeat token of index i after visiting i, and index i with A_i=1 has no repeat tokens).
- Subtlety: repeat moves of index i only become available after i is first visited. So the pool of available stalling moves grows as new indices are visited. A player wanting to stall must have some visited index with remaining count.
- This is like a game where the "stalling resource" is unlocked progressively. Need to figure out optimal play: the player who wants the game to end on their turn vs. the opponent.

Candidate framing: Let M = sum(A_i - 1). If all stalling moves could be used freely from the start, total moves = N + k where k is the number of stalls used, and the player making move N+k wins. Players effectively fight over parity: the second-to-last new-index visitor wants to force the opponent to take the last new index... Actually the player who takes the last new index WINS, so both players want to take it. So each player, when it's their turn and only one unvisited index remains, will take it and win (if a move is available — it always is since that index has A_i >= 1). So the real game: players race to be the one who takes the N-th new index. Equivalently, the player who takes the (N-1)-th new index LOSES if the opponent can immediately take the last one — opponent always can. So being the one to take the (N-1)-th new index is losing... unless you can stall with repeat moves to flip parity.

So the game reduces to: players alternately move; "new" moves decrease remaining-unvisited count; "stall" moves consume from the pool of unlocked stalls. The player who makes the N-th new move wins. This is a parity tug-of-war: define "phase" = number of unvisited indices. Each player wants to make the last new move. Standard approach: think of it as a countdown from N to 0, where each turn a player either decrements the countdown (taking a new index, also unlocking A_i - 1 new stall tokens) or consumes a stall token. The player who decrements it to 0 wins.

Pitfalls:
- Stalls are unlocked only after visiting the index, and each index's stalls are limited.
- A player might be forced to take a new index when they'd rather stall (no stall tokens available), or forced to stall when they'd rather take new index (never bad? taking new index when 1 remains wins immediately).
- Greedy parity reasoning may fail; need careful strategy analysis, possibly Sprague-Grundy-like or a clean invariant.
- Large constraints (N up to 2e5, A_i up to 1e9) demand O(N) or O(N log N) solution — likely a formula based on N, sum(A_i), and count of A_i = 1 or similar.

Hypothesis to test: the answer depends on parity of N and sum(A_i - 1), plus edge cases when stall pool is scarce early (e.g., all A_i = 1 → game is just N moves, Fennec wins iff N odd). Another test: N=1 → Fennec always wins (takes the only index). Sample 2: N=2, A=(25,29): M = 24+28 = 52. Fennec wants to take the 2nd new index. If Fennec opens with new index (say 1), then Snuke can stall 24 times on index1... parity battle over who is forced to take index 2. Total moves parity: Fennec wins iff total moves odd. Players choose stalls to control this. With huge stall pools, the player who moves second may control parity — sample says Snuke wins, consistent with "second player controls parity when stalls are abundant."
