Each swap of adjacent positions i and i+1 costs i, and sorting requires every inversion to be "resolved" by swaps. Key insight: for each pair (a,b) with a<b that is inverted (a appears to the right of b), the elements must cross, and the cheapest way to think of it is via the contribution of each adjacent boundary. Model it as: for each boundary i (between positions i and i+1), count how many elements must cross that boundary — but cost depends on which swaps happen, and each swap at boundary i costs i regardless of what's swapped. The known result for this AtCoder problem (ABC/ARC "Sort by Adjacent Swap with cost i") is: answer = sum over each element of cost to move it to its target, computed greedily — actually the clean solution: process values 1..N, and the answer equals the sum over boundaries i of i * (number of times boundary i is swapped), where the minimum number of swaps at boundary i is determined by the count of elements that must cross it, and crossings at each boundary are forced: boundary i must be swapped exactly (number of pairs crossing it) times, and since each inversion crossing boundary i requires exactly one swap there, total cost = sum over boundaries i of i * (number of inversions crossing boundary i) = sum over each inversion (a at position x, b at position y, x<y, P_x > P_y) of sum of boundary indices in [x, y-1]. So answer = sum over inversions of (sum of i for i in [pos_smaller... ]) — i.e., each inversion contributes the sum of indices between the two positions. Compute this efficiently with a Fenwick tree: iterate positions left to right, maintain BIT of positions seen; for each new element at position p with value v, the elements before it that are greater form inversions; we need sum over those of (prefix sums of boundary indices from their position to p-1). Use two BITs (count and sum of positions) to compute in O(N log N).
